Knowing how to write headlines is mostly knowing what a headline is for. It has to do four
things at once: say what this is, say who it is for, give a
reason to continue, and survive being cut short. Most headlines do the first and hope. The
method below is how to do all four deliberately.

## How to write headlines: the method

**1. Write the boring version first.** Plainly, what the thing is. "How to compress an
image for Instagram." This is your baseline and it is already better than most headlines,
because it is unambiguous.

**2. Add the specific.** A number, a constraint, a surprising detail. "…without visible
damage." Specificity is what separates a headline from a category label.

**3. Cut every word that is not doing work.** Adverbs first, then hedges, then anything
already implied.

**4. Check where it gets cut.** The most common headline failure is not being bad — it is
being truncated before the good part.

## Patterns that keep working

| Pattern | Example shape |
| --- | --- |
| How to X without Y | How to compress an image without visible damage |
| The N things that X | The five settings that suppress a video |
| X, and why Y | Shorts monetization, and why the RPM is lower |
| What X actually does | What YouTube tags actually do |
| X: the arithmetic | How much YouTubers make: the arithmetic |

These are not formulas to fill in mechanically. They work because each one promises a
specific kind of payoff — an exclusion, a count, an explanation — and a reader who wants
that payoff knows immediately.

## What to avoid

- **Curiosity with no substance.** "You won't believe what happened next" is a promise
  that the content cannot keep, and it is punished by everything that measures whether
  people stayed.
- **Cleverness that obscures.** A pun that hides the subject costs you every reader who
  was searching for the subject.
- **The category as a headline.** "Instagram Tips" is a folder name.
- **Front-loading your brand.** The first words are the most valuable ones.

## Where the truncation falls

Different everywhere, and the cut is what decides whether the good half is seen:

- [YouTube title length](/blog/youtube-title-length) — about 70 characters in search,
  fewer in a sidebar.
- [Where LinkedIn puts See more](/blog/linkedin-see-more) — two or three lines.
- [Instagram caption length](/blog/instagram-caption-length) — about 125 characters.
- A search result — roughly 580 pixels, which is not a character count.

Front-loading is the answer to all of them.

## Writing five and picking one

The habit that improves headlines faster than any technique: **never publish the first
one**. Write five, pick one, and the fifth is almost always better than the first because
the first four exhausted the obvious.

This costs about four minutes and it is the highest-return four minutes in publishing.
The reason is not mystical - the first headline you write is a description of what you
made, because that is what is in your head. The fifth is a description of what a reader
gets, because by then you have run out of ways to describe your own work.

A useful forcing function is to write the five in different shapes: one plain, one with a
number, one that names the exclusion, one that poses the question a reader would ask, and
one that states the surprising conclusion. Then pick, or take the best half of two.

## Testing a headline in ten seconds

Read it to someone who does not know the subject and ask what they think it is about. If
they hesitate, or if their answer is a category rather than a specific, the headline is not
working yet.

The second test: would you click it if you had not written it? Honest answers here are
rare and valuable. The same discipline applies to the line that closes a piece — see
[call to action examples](/blog/call-to-action-examples).

Case matters less than people think, but consistency does — see
[title case](/blog/title-case-rules) for which convention to settle on.

:::faq
Q: How do I write a good headline?
A: Write the plain version, add one specific detail, cut every word not doing work, then
check where it truncates on the platform it is for.
Q: How long should a headline be?
A: Short enough that the important half survives truncation - which is usually well under
the platform's stated limit.
Q: Do numbers in headlines work?
A: Specific numbers do, because they promise a defined amount of content. Round numbers
chosen for effect are less convincing.
Q: Should I use clickbait?
A: A promise the content keeps is not clickbait. A promise it does not keep is punished by
every metric that measures whether people stayed.
:::

A content calendar fails when it is a list of ideas with dates attached. It works when it
is a set of **recurring slots** filled from a small number of pillars, with the production
work batched — because the thing that kills a schedule is not running out of ideas, it is
context-switching.

## A content calendar starts with pillars, not ideas

Pick three or four subjects your account is actually about. Everything you publish belongs
to one of them, and consecutive posts rotate between them.

This solves two problems at once. It stops you running dry, because a pillar regenerates
where an idea list depletes. And it gives the platform a coherent picture of what you are
about, which is what determines who your work is shown to.

## Choose the cadence you can hold

The honest test: could you do this every week for six months without the work getting
worse? Most people overestimate, and the failure is expensive because an abandoned
schedule is worse than a modest one.

| Cadence | Suits |
| --- | --- |
| Weekly long-form | Most solo creators |
| 2-3 short-form per week | Anyone repurposing from long-form |
| Daily | A team, or a genuinely quick format |

## Batch by task, not by piece

The single biggest productivity gain available, and the least intuitive. Do not make one
video start to finish; do all the scripts, then all the recording, then all the edits.

A month for a weekly channel:

1. **Week one, one sitting:** choose four topics from real demand —
   [YouTube keyword research](/blog/youtube-keyword-research).
2. **Week one, second sitting:** write all four scripts. Check the runtime before
   recording.
3. **Week two:** record all four.
4. **Weeks two to four:** edit and publish one per week.

## Put the metadata in the calendar

The most common failure of a good calendar is that the piece is ready and the title,
thumbnail and description are improvised twenty minutes before publishing. Those fields
determine whether anyone sees it, so they are part of the work, not the tidying-up.

## Build repurposing into the plan

One long piece should produce several short ones, and that only happens if the slots
exist in the calendar before the piece is made.

## The three columns a calendar actually needs

Elaborate content calendars are a form of procrastination. The ones people keep using have
three columns and nothing else.

**Date.** The slot. Fixed, recurring, and not negotiable week to week - the whole value of
a calendar is that the date is decided in advance so that the only question on the day is
what goes in it.

**Pillar.** Which of your three or four subjects this slot belongs to. Assigned when the
calendar is built, not when the piece is made, so the rotation is automatic.

**Status.** Where the piece is: idea, scripted, recorded, edited, scheduled. This is the
column that makes batching visible - when six pieces sit at "scripted", you know the next
session is recording rather than writing.

Everything else people add - tags, formats, target keywords, distribution checklists -
belongs in the piece itself or in the plan, not in the calendar. A calendar with fourteen
columns is a document people stop updating in week three, and a calendar nobody updates is
worse than no calendar because it lies.

Platform documentation on scheduling tools - YouTube's own
[Creator Academy](https://creatoracademy.youtube.com/) among them - assumes you already
know what you are publishing. The calendar is the thing that makes that true.

## Review monthly, not weekly

Look at the last month and ask three questions: which pillar performed, which slot got
skipped, and what took longer than planned. Adjust the cadence rather than promising
yourself you will try harder.

The measurement side is in [vanity metrics](/blog/vanity-metrics) — a calendar reviewed
against follower count teaches you nothing, and one reviewed against a rate metric
teaches you what to make more of.

:::faq
Q: How do I make a content calendar?
A: Choose three or four pillars, decide a cadence you can sustain, lay out dated slots
that rotate the pillars, and batch the production work by task.
Q: How far ahead should a content calendar go?
A: A month of specific slots and a quarter of rough intentions. Further out than that and
you are planning fiction.
Q: What if I miss a slot?
A: Publish the next one on the normal day and say nothing. An apology costs you a slot and
tells people who did not notice.
Q: How many content pillars should I have?
A: Three or four. Fewer gets repetitive; more stops the audience knowing what the account
is about.
:::

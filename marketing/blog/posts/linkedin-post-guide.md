A LinkedIn post lives or dies on its first two lines, because that is all the feed shows
before a "see more" link. Everything else — length, format, hashtags — is downstream of
whether someone clicks to expand.

## The fold is the whole design problem

LinkedIn truncates a post after roughly two or three lines on desktop and fewer on
mobile. Expanding is a deliberate act, and it is also a signal LinkedIn measures.

## How long a LinkedIn post should be

Roughly 900 to 1,300 characters for a text post. Long enough to say something, short
enough that the expanded version does not look like homework.

Structure that works in that budget:

1. **Hook.** One line. A claim, a number, or an admission.
2. **The turn.** One line that makes the first one make sense.
3. **The substance.** Three or four short paragraphs, one idea each.
4. **The close.** The point, plainly. A question only if you genuinely want answers.

Line breaks matter more here than on any other platform: LinkedIn renders single-line
paragraphs cleanly, and a solid block of text is skipped regardless of quality.

## Formats, by how much feed space they take

| Format | Space | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Document post (PDF carousel) | Largest | Swipeable, holds attention longest |
| Native image | Large | 1200×1200 or 1080×1350 |
| Text only | Medium | Fine when the writing carries it |
| Native video | Large | Needs captions; most people watch muted |
| Link post | Smallest | The card is fixed and small |

Document posts are the format worth learning — one idea per page, large type, and a first
page that is a hook rather than a title.

## Link posts, and the comment workaround

A link post produces a small card and sends people away, both of which work against it.
The widespread workaround — image or text in the post, link in the first comment — is
unverified as a distribution lever, but it is definitely true that a native image takes
more feed space than a link card.

## Hashtags and mentions

About three hashtags, at the end —
[LinkedIn hashtags](/blog/linkedin-hashtags). Mentions are more powerful than tags on
LinkedIn because a mention notifies a person, but only mention people who are genuinely
part of the post; tagging ten people who are not is the fastest way to be muted.

## Who the audience actually is

The single most useful thing to understand about LinkedIn is that the audience is not
your connections. It is your connections' feeds, plus whoever follows the hashtags you
used, plus - crucially - the people who see the post because someone they know commented
on it.

That last route is why comments matter disproportionately here. A comment does not just
signal engagement to the ranking system; it physically surfaces the post to a new set of
people. A post with twenty thoughtful comments has been shown to twenty extended
networks.

Two things follow, and they are the opposite of what most posting advice suggests.

**Write things people can respond to substantively.** Not "agree?" - a claim specific
enough that a peer has something to add. Practitioners commenting with their own
experience is the mechanism working correctly.

**Reply to every comment, properly.** A reply is another comment, it keeps the thread
active, and on a professional network it is also just good manners with a direct
distribution benefit attached.

## What actually gets distributed

Engagement in the first hour, and dwell time. Both favour posts that are worth expanding
and reading rather than posts that are optimised for a click.

Two things follow. **Consistency beats volume** — two considered posts a week outperform
daily filler, because credibility is the currency on a professional network. And
**comments are worth more than likes**, both as a signal and because a comment thread
brings a post back into feeds.

:::faq
Q: How long should a LinkedIn post be?
A: Around 900-1,300 characters for a text post, with the hook in the first two lines
because that is all the feed shows before "see more".
Q: What is the best format on LinkedIn?
A: Document posts take the most feed space and hold attention longest. Native images come
second; link posts take the least space.
Q: Should I put links in the comments on LinkedIn?
A: A native post plus a link in the first comment takes more feed space than a link post.
Whether it changes distribution is unverified.
Q: How often should I post on LinkedIn?
A: Two or three times a week is sustainable and enough. Credibility falls faster than
reach when the posts thin out.
:::

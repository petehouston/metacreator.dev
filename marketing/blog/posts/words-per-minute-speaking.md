Words per minute speaking is roughly 130 to 150 for conversational delivery, 110 to 130
for careful explanation, and 160 or more for energetic short-form. Which means a
two-minute video is somewhere between 220 and 320 words — a range wide enough that
guessing is how scripts end up twice their intended length.

## Words per minute speaking, by delivery

| Delivery | Words per minute | 60 seconds | 5 minutes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Slow and deliberate | 110-130 | ~120 words | ~600 words |
| Conversational | 130-150 | ~140 words | ~700 words |
| Energetic short-form | 150-170 | ~160 words | ~800 words |

[[tool:script-timer]]

The script timer converts a word count to a runtime at a speed you set, which is more
useful than an average because your own pace is what matters. Record 60 seconds of natural
speech once, count the words, and you have your number for good.

## Why this matters before recording

A script that runs long is discovered at one of two moments: before recording, when it
costs five minutes to fix, or during editing, when it costs an hour and the cuts damage
the structure.

It also matters because format length is a decision with consequences.

**Short-form** lives on completion. A 45-second video that people finish outperforms a
75-second one they abandon — [TikTok growth](/blog/tiktok-growth-guide).

**Long-form** is judged on watch time rather than length, but padding is punished
immediately in the retention graph.

## Pacing is not just speed

Two scripts of identical length can feel very different, and words per minute only
captures part of it:

- **Pauses.** A deliberate pause costs a second and buys emphasis. Scripts that read fast
  often play slow because the delivery needs air.
- **Complexity.** Dense material needs a slower delivery and therefore fewer words per
  minute — see [readability score](/blog/readability-score), which is a proxy for the
  same thing.
- **Visuals.** Anything the viewer needs to look at needs time in which nothing is said.

## Working backwards from a slot

If your format has a fixed length — a 60-second Short, a 3-minute explainer — write to the
word count rather than trimming afterwards. Trimming a finished script removes the parts
that were easiest to cut, which are rarely the parts that should go.

[[tool:word-counter]]

Word count first, structure second, sentences last. That order produces a script that fits
and still has a shape.

## Reading aloud is the real test

No formula catches a sentence that is difficult to say. Reading a script aloud finds the
clause that trips you, the word you keep mispronouncing and the paragraph with no breath in
it — all of which cost takes.

Do it once before recording. It is the cheapest quality improvement available to anyone who
speaks on camera, and the University of Michigan's
[research on speech rate](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/) is a reminder that
comprehension falls off well before the speaker notices.

:::faq
Q: How many words per minute do people speak?
A: Around 130 to 150 conversationally, slower for careful explanation and faster for
energetic short-form delivery.
Q: How many words is a 60-second video?
A: Roughly 120 to 160 depending on pace. Measure your own rate once and use that instead
of an average.
Q: How long should a script be for a 10-minute video?
A: Around 1,300 to 1,500 words at a conversational pace, less if there is a lot to look at
on screen.
Q: Does reading speed differ from speaking speed?
A: Yes - silent reading is considerably faster. Never estimate a runtime from how long the
script takes you to read in your head.
:::

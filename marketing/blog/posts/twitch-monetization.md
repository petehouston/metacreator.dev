Twitch monetization runs on four streams: subscriptions, bits, ads and direct tips.
Twitch publishes the arithmetic for three of them — a Tier 1 sub is $4.99, the standard
split is 50/50, a bit is worth a cent — so most of the guesswork people bring to this
question is unnecessary.

## The two programmes Twitch monetization runs through

**Affiliate** is the first rung, and it is where subscriptions, bits and ads switch on.
The requirements are a follower threshold plus a small amount of recent streaming
activity, and Twitch lists them in its
[Affiliate programme documentation](https://www.twitch.tv/p/en/partners/affiliates/).

**Partner** is the second. It brings more control — more ad options, more emote slots,
and for some partners a better subscription split — but the income streams themselves
are the same four. A channel does not gain a new way to make money by becoming a
Partner; it gains better terms on the ways it already had.

The practical read: Affiliate is the milestone that matters. Everything below is
available at it.

## Subscriptions: the half that is really yours

A Tier 1 subscription is $4.99. Tier 2 is $9.99 and Tier 3 is $24.99, and both are rare
enough that a channel's average sub price sits very close to Tier 1 unless it has an
unusual audience.

The standard split is **50/50**. Some Partners on older premium terms keep 70%, and that
is the difference the second option in the calculator exists for. Prime subscriptions
pay the streamer the same as a Tier 1 and cost the viewer nothing extra, because the
cost is bundled into their Amazon Prime membership.

Two consequences worth internalising:

- **A hundred subs is about $250 a month**, not $499. Half of the headline figure is the
  platform's.
- **Prime subs are more volatile.** They renew only when the viewer re-subscribes each
  month, so a Prime-heavy channel's sub count moves more than a paid-heavy one's.

## Bits and tips: the two extremes of the fee question

A **bit** is worth exactly one cent to the channel it is cheered in. Twitch takes its
cut on the viewer's side, when they buy the bits, which is why the streamer-side
arithmetic is so simple: ten thousand bits is a hundred dollars.

A **direct tip** — through a third-party payment page — is not split at all. Twitch takes
nothing, and that is why so many channels route tipping off-platform. The trade is that
you handle the payment processor's fees, the chargebacks and the tax paperwork yourself,
and the tip does not earn the viewer any of the on-platform status a sub or a cheer
does.

Neither is predictable enough to plan around. Both are worth having.

## Ads: the visible income and the smaller half

Ads are the stream people assume is the big one, and on most channels they are not
close.

Ad revenue is paid on impressions, and an impression is one viewer seeing one ad. So the
figure is average viewers multiplied by hours streamed multiplied by ad minutes per
hour — and then a CPM that Twitch does not publish and that moves with audience country
and time of year.

That unpublished CPM is the one number in this whole subject that is genuinely an
estimate. Any calculator that hides it behind a default and shows you a confident total
is inventing your income. Ours puts it in the form, with the default marked as a
starting point rather than a fact.

The comparison to run once you have both figures: subscriptions against a full month of
ad breaks. On most channels subs win, often by several times, and that answers a
question streamers ask constantly — whether to run more ads or to ask for the sub. Work
it out for your own numbers with the
[Twitch money calculator](/tools/twitch-money-calculator).

## Sponsorships, and why no calculator quotes them

There is a fifth stream, and it is the largest one on most channels above a few hundred
concurrent viewers: brand deals. It is missing from the arithmetic above for a reason
worth stating rather than glossing.

Sponsorship money is negotiated privately, priced on reach and audience rather than on
any published rate, and varies by an order of magnitude between two channels with
identical viewer counts in different categories. No calculator can quote it honestly,
and the ones that try are producing a number from a formula somebody invented.

What you can do is price it the way a brand does — average concurrent viewers times a
category rate, adjusted for how engaged the chat is — which is the same logic that
prices a sponsored post anywhere else. Start from your own engagement figures rather
than from a follower count.

## The failure mode: counting the headline number

Almost every "I make X on Twitch" figure you will read is a pre-split number, and the
gap is not small — half of every subscription dollar, on the standard terms.

The second failure is counting a good month as the rate. Sub counts fall in any month a
channel streams less, because a meaningful share of subs are Prime subs that have to be
manually renewed. A channel that takes two weeks off does not lose two weeks of income;
it loses a slice of the recurring base and spends the next month rebuilding it.

Both are why the per-hour figure at the bottom of the calculator is the one to watch. It
is unaffected by a good month, and it goes up only when the channel gets better rather
than longer.

The one figure that puts all of it in perspective is revenue per hour streamed. It is

the evening, and it is the number nobody calculates.

:::faq
Q: How much does a Twitch sub pay the streamer?
A: Half of $4.99 on the standard split — roughly $2.50 — before tax. Partners on older premium terms keep 70%.
Q: How much is a bit worth?
A: One cent to the channel. Twitch takes its margin when the viewer buys the bits, not when they are cheered.
Q: Do Prime subs pay the same as paid subs?
A: Yes, at the Tier 1 rate. They are less stable, because the viewer has to re-subscribe each month for one to renew.
Q: Is ad revenue worth chasing?
A: It is worth setting deliberately. On most channels it is the smaller half of the income and the larger half of the viewer cost, which is a trade to make on purpose.
:::

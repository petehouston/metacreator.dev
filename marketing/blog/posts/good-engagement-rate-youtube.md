YouTube engagement rate is (likes + comments) ÷ views × 100, and a typical figure is
between 2% and 5%. It is worth calculating for comparison with other creators, and it is
genuinely less important than click-through rate, audience retention and watch time —
none of which appear in it.

## Calculating a YouTube engagement rate

| Formula | Use |
| --- | --- |
| (likes + comments) ÷ views × 100 | The externally verifiable version, per video |
| (likes + comments + shares) ÷ views × 100 | Your own figure, if you have Studio access |
| (likes + comments) ÷ subscribers × 100 | Comparing channels of similar size |

Bands, by channel size, using the views-based formula:

| Subscribers | Weak | Typical | Strong |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Under 10,000 | Under 2% | 3-6% | Above 6% |
| 10,000-100,000 | Under 1.5% | 2-5% | Above 5% |
| Over 100,000 | Under 1% | 1.5-3% | Above 3% |

As everywhere, these are directional bands from public samples rather than published
figures — see [social media benchmarks](/blog/social-media-benchmarks) for the method
and the caveats.

## Why it matters less on YouTube

On Instagram or TikTok, engagement is the main visible signal of whether a post landed.
On YouTube it is a by-product, because YouTube optimises for something it can measure
much better: **whether people keep watching**.

The three numbers that actually drive distribution:

**Click-through rate.** Of the people shown your thumbnail, how many clicked. This is
where most underperforming videos lose, and it is a title and thumbnail problem —
[YouTube title length](/blog/youtube-title-length) and
[YouTube thumbnail size](/blog/youtube-thumbnail-size).

**Audience retention.** What share of the video people watch, and where they leave. The
first thirty seconds is where the promise made by the title is kept or broken.

**Watch time.** Retention multiplied by length multiplied by views — and the currency of
the Partner Programme threshold as well:
[YouTube monetization requirements](/blog/youtube-monetization-requirements).

## What engagement rate is still good for

**Comparing yourself to other channels**, because likes and comments are public and
retention is not. If you are researching a niche, engagement rate is the only
performance signal you can compute from outside.

**Spotting a mismatch.** High views and very low engagement often means the video was
recommended to a broad audience that did not care — which is worth knowing before you
make another one like it.

**Comments specifically.** A comment is the expensive interaction. A video with an
unusually high comment ratio has touched something, and reading those comments is
better research than any metric.

## Reading the numbers together

No single metric diagnoses a video. The pairs are what tell you something, and there are
only four combinations worth memorising.

**High CTR, low retention.** The title and thumbnail promised something the video did not
deliver. This is the most damaging combination, because YouTube gave the video a chance
and the viewers rejected it - and the fix is usually to change the video, not the packaging.

**Low CTR, high retention.** The video is good and nobody is clicking it. The cheapest fix
in this entire subject: rewrite the title, redo the thumbnail, and let YouTube retest it.

**Low CTR, low retention.** The subject is wrong for the audience it is being shown to.
Go back to what people actually search for rather than adjusting the packaging.

**High both.** Make more of these. Genuinely - the most under-used analytics action is
looking at what already worked and doing it again rather than moving on to something new.

Engagement rate sits alongside those pairs as a secondary signal. It rarely changes the
diagnosis, but a very low comment count on a video with strong retention often means the
video answered the question so completely that there was nothing to ask - which is not a
problem.

## What not to conclude from a low number

A falling engagement rate as a channel grows is normal — the same pattern as every other
platform, for the same reason: the audience broadens faster than enthusiasm does.

And a video with poor engagement but strong retention is a good video. The reverse — high
likes and terrible retention — is the one to worry about, because YouTube counts the
second and viewers remember it.

If a video simply is not being shown at all, that is a different problem with a
documented checklist:
[YouTube video not showing in search](/blog/youtube-video-not-showing-in-search), and
YouTube's own account of how it recommends is in its
[Creator Academy](https://creatoracademy.youtube.com/).

:::faq
Q: What is a good YouTube engagement rate?
A: Roughly 3-6% of views for smaller channels and 1.5-3% for large ones, using likes
plus comments divided by views.
Q: Is engagement rate important on YouTube?
A: Less than on other platforms. Click-through rate, retention and watch time drive
distribution; engagement is a by-product.
Q: What is a good like-to-view ratio on YouTube?
A: Around 3-5% is common. Very low ratios on a high-view video often mean it reached a
broad, uninterested audience.
Q: Do comments matter more than likes?
A: As a signal to you, yes - a comment costs effort, so a high comment ratio means
something landed. Both are minor next to retention.
:::

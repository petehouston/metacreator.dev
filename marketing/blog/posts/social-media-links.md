Social media links are rewritten before anyone clicks them. The platform wraps or
shortens the URL, strips the part it does not want to show, appends its own tracking
identifier, and draws a card from the destination's meta tags. Four decisions are
yours: which form of the URL you post, whether you shorten it, what you tag it with,
and what the card says.

This guide covers all four. The short version is that the platform's own domain beats
a third-party shortener, tracking parameters belong on the destination rather than in
the link you paste, and the card is worth checking on a phone before you post.

## Which platforms have their own short link domain

A first-party short link is served by the platform itself. It never expires, needs no
account, and cannot break because a shortening service changed its pricing. The field
splits cleanly in two, and the split is not the one most articles describe.

| Platform | Short domain | Can you build one from a URL? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| YouTube | `youtu.be` | **Yes** — the video ID is the path |
| Instagram | `instagr.am` | **Yes** — legacy alias domain, still redirects |
| Reddit | `redd.it` | **Yes** — the post ID from `/comments/` |
| Dailymotion | `dai.ly` | **Yes** |
| Flickr | `flic.kr` | **Yes** — the photo ID in base58 |
| Telegram | `t.me` | **Yes** — already the canonical domain |
| WhatsApp | `wa.me` | **Yes** — from a phone number |
| X | `t.co` | No — applied automatically at post time |
| LinkedIn | `lnkd.in` | No — issued by the share button |
| Pinterest | `pin.it` | No — issued by the app's share sheet |
| Facebook | `fb.me`, `fb.watch` | No — generated when you share from the app |
| TikTok | `vm.tiktok.com` | No — generated by the app's share sheet |

YouTube's is the one most people need, and it has a trap in it: a watch page writes a
timestamp as `t=90s`, a `youtu.be` link wants bare seconds, and copying the parameter
across unchanged starts the video from the beginning without complaining —
[how to shorten a YouTube link](/blog/shorten-a-youtube-link) covers it.

The right-hand column is the part worth internalising. Any site offering you a "TikTok
link shortener" or an "X link shortener" is a **third-party redirector** wearing the
platform's name. Your link then depends on somebody else's server staying up, and on
their analytics staying honest about where it sent people.

## What a redirect chain costs

Every hop is a full round trip before anything renders. On a phone with a slow
connection that is a visible delay, and each hop is an opportunity for something to
break: a shortener that expires, a redirect that drops your query string, a chain that
loops.

Chains also hide things. A link with three hops in it tells the person clicking nothing
about where they are going, which is precisely why phishing uses shorteners. If you
receive a link and cannot see the destination, expand it first —
[how to expand a short URL](/blog/expand-a-short-url) covers the mechanics and the
two failure modes worth knowing about.

On a link you control, three or more hops is worth collapsing. The usual culprit is a
shortener pointing at a marketing redirect pointing at a page whose canonical URL
differs by a trailing slash.

## The tracking parameters platforms add for themselves

Copy a link out of a share sheet and it usually arrives carrying something extra:

- `si` on YouTube
- `igshid` and `igsh` on Instagram
- `share_id`, `is_from_webapp` and `sender_device` on TikTok
- `fbclid` on Facebook

These are **per-share identifiers**, not analytics you can read. Forwarding a link that
still carries one tells the platform who forwarded it to whom. Strip them before you
publish a link anywhere. Both shorteners on this site do it by default and list what
they removed.

Your own tracking is a separate matter, and it belongs on the destination URL as UTM
parameters rather than inside a redirect service — see
[UTM parameters for creators](/blog/utm-parameters-for-creators). The one exception
worth respecting: on YouTube, `t` is a timestamp, not a tracker. Strip it and the video
starts from the beginning.

## The card the feed draws

Once posted, the link stops being a URL and becomes a card: an image, a title, a domain
and — on some platforms — a description. All of it is read from the destination's Open
Graph tags, and each platform crops it differently. Facebook shows roughly 88 characters
of title on desktop and about 65 on a phone; LinkedIn shows 100 and about 60, and
ignores the description entirely.

That gap is why a preview that looks fine at your desk can be cut mid-word for most of
the people who see it. [Why your link preview looks different on
mobile](/blog/link-preview-mobile-vs-desktop) works through it, and
[Open Graph tags](/blog/open-graph-tags) covers the tags themselves. Meta documents its
own sharing behaviour in the
[Sharing best practices](https://developers.facebook.com/docs/sharing/best-practices/)
guide.

## Cleaning a link, and making it open in the app

Two jobs sit either side of the shortener, and both have their own page.

**Cleaning.** The parameters a share sheet attaches are not all campaign tags: `igshid`
identifies the account that shared, `mc_eid` identifies the newsletter subscriber, and
both travel with a forwarded link. Equally, a cleaner that strips YouTube's `t` has
broken the link it was asked to fix.
[Removing tracking parameters](/blog/remove-tracking-parameters-from-url) covers what
each one is and which four to keep.

**Opening in the app.** A universal link — the ordinary `https://` URL — opens the app
when it is installed and the website when it is not. A scheme URI like
`instagram://user?username=` opens the app or does nothing at all, silently, which makes
it the wrong choice for anything a stranger might tap.
[Social media deep links](/blog/social-media-deep-links) has both forms and the schemes
worth relying on.

## Four decisions to make about every social media link

1. **Post the canonical URL**, not the one your browser happened to be on. A watch page
   with a playlist and an index parameter is not the link you meant to share.
2. **Shorten only with the platform's own domain**, or not at all. A third-party
   shortener buys you click counts and costs you permanence.
3. **Tag the destination, not the redirect.** UTM parameters on the landing page survive
   every hop; parameters on a shortener do not.
4. **Check the card on mobile** before you post it, not after.

:::tip
If you only do one of these, do the fourth. A cut title is the failure people actually
see, and it is the one that costs clicks on every impression rather than a few.
:::

:::faq
Q: Is a short link bad for SEO?
A: No. A 301 from a platform's own domain passes authority normally. The concern people have in mind is a chain of third-party redirects, which adds latency and can break — which is an argument for a first-party short link, not against short links.
Q: Should I use a link shortener at all?
A: Only where character count genuinely matters, or where the platform's own domain is the shortener. A branded third-party shortener is a reasonable choice for a business that needs click data; it is a poor one for a link that has to still work in five years.
Q: Do UTM parameters show up in the link preview?
A: No. Every platform shows the bare domain in a card, never the path or the query string. A carefully tagged link renders as plain "example.com".
Q: Can I edit where a short link points after I have shared it?
A: With a third-party shortener, usually yes — which is also why an expanded link is only true at the moment you expand it. First-party short links point at one object permanently and cannot be repointed.
:::

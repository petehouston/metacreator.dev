Most calls to action fail for one reason: they ask before they have earned anything. A CTA
is the last part of a trade, and if the first part did not happen, no phrasing rescues it.
These call to action examples assume the trade happened.

## The structure

Three parts, in this order:

1. **What they get.** The benefit, stated concretely.
2. **What to do.** One action, unambiguous.
3. **How little it costs.** Free, no account, ten seconds — whatever is true.

"See where your title gets cut off — paste it into the counter, no account needed" has all
three. "Check out our tool!" has none.

## Call to action examples, by goal

| Goal | Weak | Better |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Comment | "Thoughts?" | "Which of these three do you actually use?" |
| Save | "Save this!" | "Save this for the next time a preview breaks" |
| Follow | "Follow for more" | "I post one teardown a week — follow if that is useful" |
| Click | "Link in bio" | "The full checklist is in the bio link" |
| Signup | "Subscribe to my newsletter" | "One email a week, the same thing in writing" |
| Share | "Share with a friend" | "Send this to whoever set up your analytics" |

The pattern in the right column: every one names *who it is for* or *when to use it*. That
specificity is what turns an instruction into a suggestion someone can accept.

## Placement matters more than wording

**Put it where the value lands**, not at the end. The moment someone realises the thing you
described solves their problem is the moment to tell them where it is. On a page, that is
mid-article; in a video, it is right after the payoff.

**One per piece.** Two calls to action is a choice, and a choice is friction.

**Not at the start.** A CTA before any value has been delivered is a request from a
stranger.

That is also why our own posts put a tool card at the point of use rather than parking it
at the bottom — the same principle applies to your captions.

## Platform notes

**Instagram.** The caption fold cuts early, so a CTA at the end is read only by people who
expanded — which is fine, because those are the people who might act.
[Instagram caption length](/blog/instagram-caption-length).

**LinkedIn.** A question that peers can answer from experience outperforms any other CTA
here, because comments drive distribution —
[LinkedIn post length, formats and reach](/blog/linkedin-post-guide).

**YouTube.** The ask belongs after the payoff, in one sentence, and it should name the next
video — [YouTube description template](/blog/youtube-description-template).

**TikTok.** The caption is short and covers your video, so the CTA is usually a spoken line
or the last frame — [TikTok caption length](/blog/tiktok-caption-length).

:::faq
Q: What makes a good call to action?
A: Naming what the reader gets, one unambiguous action, and an honest statement of what it
costs them.
Q: Where should a CTA go in a post?
A: Where the value lands rather than at the end - the moment someone realises the thing
solves their problem.
Q: How many calls to action should one post have?
A: One. Two is a choice, and a choice is friction.
Q: Why does "link in bio" perform badly?
A: It is an instruction with no benefit attached. Naming what is at the link converts far
better than pointing at it.
:::

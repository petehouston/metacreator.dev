A Pinterest board strategy matters because board names and descriptions are text
Pinterest reads, and every Pin saved to a board inherits that context. A board called
"Small kitchen storage" helps every Pin on it; one called "Bits I like" helps none of
them and dilutes the account.

## A Pinterest board strategy starts with names

Name the board the way someone would search for its contents. Two or three words,
descriptive, no wordplay:

| Instead of | Use |
| --- | --- |
| Yum | Weeknight dinner recipes |
| Inspo | Small bathroom design |
| My stuff | Freelance business templates |
| Reading | Non-fiction book recommendations |

Add a board description of a sentence or two containing the same subject in natural
words. It is read, it costs nothing, and almost nobody writes one.

## How many boards

Fewer than you think. A board only accumulates authority on a subject if Pins keep
arriving on it, and thirty boards with four Pins each is thirty weak signals.

A working structure for most accounts:

- **Three to eight subject boards**, each one a real topic you publish about regularly.
- **Sections inside them** for sub-topics, rather than new boards.
- **One board for your own content** only if it genuinely helps a visitor; the subject
  boards should already contain it.

Sections are the underused feature here. They subdivide a board without splitting its
accumulated signal, which is exactly what you want when "Small kitchen storage" needs to
distinguish shelving from drawers.

## Where boards fit into ranking

Board context is one input among several, and the Pin's own fields carry more weight:

1. **Pin title** — the query, front-loaded.
2. **Pin description** — prose for matching, not shown in feeds —
   [writing a Pin description](/blog/pinterest-pin-description).
3. **Board name and description** — subject context.
4. **Destination page** — read, and it should match the promise.

## Building boards ahead of the content

Because Pinterest is search-driven and seasonal, boards benefit from being created before
you need them rather than in response to a Pin you have just made.

The practical version: decide the three to eight subjects your account is about, create
those boards with real names and descriptions, and then produce Pins into them. That
sounds obvious and is the opposite of how most accounts grow - they accumulate Pins first
and organise later, by which point the early Pins are sitting on a catch-all board and
carrying no context.

Seasonality is the second reason to plan boards early. Pinterest searches run weeks to
months ahead of the event, so a board that will matter in December should exist, with Pins
on it, well before then. A board created the week of the event has no accumulated signal
at the moment it needs one.

## Things that quietly hurt

- **Renaming boards frequently.** The accumulated context resets.
- **Group boards with no moderation.** Once they fill with unrelated Pins the board's
  subject signal is gone.
- **Saving everything to one catch-all board.** It tells Pinterest nothing.
- **Boards for seasons rather than subjects.** "Christmas" is a season; "Christmas table
  settings" is a subject.

:::faq
Q: Do Pinterest board names affect SEO?
A: Yes. Board names and descriptions are read by Pinterest and provide context for every
Pin saved to them.
Q: How many Pinterest boards should I have?
A: Three to eight real subject boards, subdivided with sections rather than split into
more boards.
Q: Should I use sections or new boards?
A: Sections, in most cases. They subdivide without splitting the board's accumulated
subject signal.
Q: Are group boards still worth joining?
A: Only well-moderated ones on a single subject. An unmoderated group board loses its
topic signal quickly.
:::

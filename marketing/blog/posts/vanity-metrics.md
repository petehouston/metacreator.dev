A vanity metric is a number that reliably goes up, feels like progress, and does not
predict anything you care about. Follower count is the archetype: it only ever rises, it
is easy to inflate, and it tells you nothing about whether anyone will watch your next
video or buy anything.

## The usual suspects

| Metric | Why it flatters | What it hides |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Follower count | Monotonic — it rarely falls | How many still watch |
| Total views, all time | Grows forever | Whether recent work lands |
| Likes | Cheap to give | Whether anyone acted |
| Impressions | Large and impressive | Click-through, which is the real question |
| Subscriber count on a dormant channel | Historic | Present reach |

None of these is fake. They are simply *lagging, cumulative and cheap*, and those three
properties make a number comfortable rather than useful.

## The test

Ask two questions of any metric:

1. **Can it go down?** A number that only rises cannot tell you that something is wrong,
   which means it cannot guide a decision.
2. **Does it cost the audience anything?** A like costs nothing. A save, a share, a
   comment, a subscription, a purchase all cost something — attention, reputation or
   money — and are therefore evidence.

Metrics that pass both tests are worth a dashboard. Metrics that fail both belong in a
screenshot for your parents.

## What to track instead

**Rate metrics, not totals.** Engagement rate rather than likes; click-through rate
rather than impressions. A rate can fall, which is what makes it informative —
[engagement rate](/blog/engagement-rate).

[[tool:engagement-rate-calculator]]

**Retention and completion.** The most honest number any platform gives a creator,
because it measures whether the thing was worth watching. On YouTube it outranks every
public metric — [YouTube engagement rate](/blog/good-engagement-rate-youtube).

**Saves and shares.** The interactions that cost something.

**Recent-window performance.** Median engagement over the last twelve posts, compared
with the twelve before. This is the number that answers "am I getting better", which
totals never can.

**Something outside the platform.** Newsletter signups, clicks to a site, enquiries. One
tracked link is worth more than a month of platform analytics because it survives the
platform.

## The special case of follower count

It is not worthless — it is a threshold metric. Crossing 1,000 subscribers unlocks the
Partner Programme; crossing some number makes a brand return your email. Thresholds
matter.

What it is not is a measure of health. Two accounts with 50,000 followers can have wildly
different reach, and the difference shows up immediately in a rate metric. If you track
one number alongside followers, make it engagement rate over a recent window —
[social media benchmarks](/blog/social-media-benchmarks) has the bands by size.

## Where vanity metrics do real damage

**In pricing.** A creator who quotes follower count and a brand who pays on it are both
guessing. The brand finds out afterwards, and there is no second campaign.

**In direction.** Optimising a vanity metric changes what you make. Chasing views leads
to broader, blander subjects; chasing saves leads to more useful ones. Those are
different channels within a year.

**In morale.** A number that only goes up cannot tell you that you are improving, so it
stops meaning anything - and then a flat week feels like failure for no measurable reason.

Definitions for every term used here are in
[the creator metrics glossary](/blog/creator-glossary), and platform documentation on what
each metric counts is worth reading once - Meta's
[Business Help Centre](https://www.facebook.com/business/help) and YouTube's
[Creator Academy](https://creatoracademy.youtube.com/) both define their terms, and the
definitions are less obvious than the words suggest.

:::faq
Q: What are vanity metrics?
A: Numbers that reliably rise, feel like progress, and do not predict outcomes -
follower count, all-time views and likes are the common ones.
Q: Are followers a vanity metric?
A: Mostly, with one exception: they act as thresholds for programme eligibility and for
brand interest. As a measure of health they are weak.
Q: What should creators track instead?
A: Rate metrics that can fall - engagement rate, click-through rate, retention - plus
saves, shares, and one number outside the platform such as newsletter signups.
Q: Are views a vanity metric?
A: All-time views are. Views on recent work, compared with earlier recent work, are a
real signal.
:::

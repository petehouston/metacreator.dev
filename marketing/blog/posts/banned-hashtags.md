Banned hashtags are tags a platform has restricted, usually because they attracted rule-
breaking content. Using one can stop a post appearing under any of its tags, and nothing
tells you it happened. The good news is that all three failure modes — banned,
restricted and simply dead — can be checked in about a minute each.

## Banned hashtags, restricted tags and dead tags

| Failure | What you see | What it costs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Banned** | The tag page shows no recent posts, or a warning about community guidelines | The post may be suppressed under all its tags |
| **Restricted** | The tag page shows top posts but no recent feed | Reduced discovery through that tag |
| **Dead** | The tag exists with almost no posts or activity | Nothing, except a wasted slot |

Only the first is genuinely harmful. The third is merely useless, and is far more
common than the first — most tags people worry about are simply not used by anyone.

## How to check a tag before using it

1. **Open the tag in the app**, not in a browser. Platforms show restriction states
   differently to logged-in mobile users.
2. **Look for the recent feed.** A tag showing top posts but no recent posts is
   restricted.
3. **Read the warning, if any.** Some platforms show an explicit community-guidelines
   notice.
4. **Check the post count.** A tag with a few hundred posts is not a distribution
   channel, whatever else it is.

## Why an innocent tag gets restricted

Tags are restricted because of the content posted under them, not because of the words.
Ordinary, harmless words get caught regularly when they become shorthand for something
that breaks the rules — which is why a tag can be perfectly safe for a year and
restricted the next month, with no announcement.

Two implications:

- **Re-check the tags you reuse.** A saved set is a liability if you never look at it
  again. Quarterly is enough.
- **Do not assume the word explains it.** Innocuous single words are among the most
  frequently restricted, because they are the most frequently misused.

## If you think a post was suppressed

Work through it in order, because the tag is rarely the actual cause:

1. **Check whether the post is visible to a logged-out viewer.** If it is, the problem
   is reach, not suppression.
2. **Check your own account status.** Platforms notify account-level restrictions in
   their settings; look there before theorising.
3. **Remove the suspect tag** and see whether the next post behaves normally.
4. **Check the platform-specific settings** that suppress content outright. On YouTube
   that is a documented list —
   [YouTube video not showing in search](/blog/youtube-video-not-showing-in-search).

## The proportion worth keeping in mind

Hashtags are a modest signal everywhere and a cliff-edge risk in one place — YouTube
ignores every hashtag on a video carrying more than 60, and says over-tagging may remove
a video from search altogether
([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6390658)).

Everywhere else, a bad tag costs you a slot rather than a post. Which means the correct
amount of anxiety about banned hashtags is: check them occasionally, use fewer of them,
and spend the saved worry on the hook. The per-network rules are in
[how to use hashtags](/blog/how-to-use-hashtags), and the Instagram specifics in
[Instagram hashtags](/blog/instagram-hashtags).

:::faq
Q: How do I know if a hashtag is banned?
A: Open it in the app. A banned or restricted tag shows no recent posts, and sometimes
a community-guidelines notice, even though top posts may still appear.
Q: Can one banned hashtag hurt a whole post?
A: On some platforms a restricted tag can reduce the post's discoverability under all
its tags. Removing it and posting normally afterwards is the test.
Q: Do banned hashtags change over time?
A: Constantly. Tags are restricted because of the content posted under them, so a safe
tag can become restricted without notice.
Q: Is a hashtag with few posts bad?
A: Not harmful, just useless as distribution. It is a label rather than a channel.
:::

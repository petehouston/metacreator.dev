How to use hashtags depends entirely on the network. Instagram allows 30 per post and
treats them as topic signals; YouTube shows 3 above your title and ignores every
hashtag on a video carrying more than 60; LinkedIn's working convention is about three.
Applying one platform's habits to another is the most common hashtag mistake there is.

## How to use hashtags: the rules, per network

| Network | Documented limit | What works | Where they go |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Instagram | 30 per post | 3-8, mixed reach levels | Caption or first comment |
| YouTube | 60 before all are ignored | 3-5 | Description |
| TikTok | Caption character limit | 3-5, specific | Caption |
| LinkedIn | No documented cap | ~3 | End of the post |
| X | None | 1-2 | In the sentence |
| Pinterest | — | Effectively none; use keywords | Description as prose |
| Facebook | None | 1-2 | End of the post |

Sources: [Instagram Help](https://help.instagram.com/351460621611097) on tags, and
[YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6390658) on hashtags.

Two of those rows are worth their own emphasis. **YouTube's over-60 rule is a cliff**,
not a taper — past it, none of them count. And **Pinterest is not a hashtag platform at
all**; it is a keyword search engine, and prose beats tags there.

## What a hashtag actually does

Less than most people assume, and it varies:

- **A topic signal.** On Instagram and TikTok, tags help categorise a post for
  recommendation. They are one input among many, and they do not rescue a post nobody
  engages with.
- **A browsable surface.** People can tap a hashtag and see the feed for it. Real, and
  a small fraction of most posts' reach.
- **A community marker.** `#booktok`, `#buildinpublic` — this is where hashtags earn
  their keep, by putting a post in front of a self-selecting group.

What a hashtag does not do is add reach in proportion to how many you use. Thirty tags
does not mean thirty times the reach, and on YouTube it means zero.

## The mix that works

On the platforms where hashtags do function as topic signals, a good set is layered by
size:

1. **One or two broad.** Large, competitive, describes the category. Your post will not
   be found here, but it categorises you.
2. **Three or four mid-sized.** Specific enough that your post can surface, big enough
   that people browse them.
3. **One or two narrow.** Niche or community tags where you can genuinely be seen.

The mid tier does the work. Broad tags are a lottery ticket; narrow tags are a small
but real audience; the middle is where a post with modest engagement can actually
appear.

## Building a set you reuse

Researching hashtags for every post is how people give up on them. The alternative is a
small library, built once and revised occasionally.

Make three or four **sets**, one per content pillar - the recurring subjects your
account is actually about. Each set has its broad, mid and niche tags already chosen.
When you post, you pick the set that matches, adjust one or two tags for the specific
post, and publish.

This has two advantages beyond saving time. It keeps your tagging consistent, which
helps the recommendation system understand what your account is about rather than
seeing a different vocabulary every day. And it makes the tags reviewable: a set you
can see is a set you can improve when something stops working.

Revise the sets quarterly, not weekly. Hashtag performance moves slowly, and reacting
to a single quiet post produces churn rather than insight.

## A note on hashtags in bios and profiles

Clickable on most networks, and largely ineffective as a discovery mechanism. A hashtag
in a bio does not place your profile in that hashtag's feed - it just gives a visitor
somewhere else to go, which is rarely what you want on the page where they are deciding
to follow you.

The exception is a genuine community marker that signals belonging to a visitor. That
is a positioning decision rather than a discovery one, and it belongs in
[bio copy](/blog/instagram-bio-ideas) rather than in a hashtag strategy.

## Reading somebody else's hashtag set

The fastest way to understand a tag strategy is to look at one that is working. Pulling
the tags off a competitor's post takes seconds — [how to find the hashtags on a
post](/blog/find-hashtags-on-a-post) covers the mechanics, including the platforms that
answer with a sign-in wall and what to do instead.

Read the set for its shape rather than copying it. Their tags were chosen for their
audience size, and a set built around ten-million-post tags will bury a small account.

## When hashtags stop working

A tag can be broken, restricted or simply dead, and none of that is announced. If posts
using a particular tag never appear under it, the tag itself may be the problem rather
than the post — the diagnostic order is in
[banned hashtags](/blog/banned-hashtags).

## The thing that outranks hashtags everywhere

On every network in that table, engagement in the first hour matters more than the tag
set. Hashtags help a good post find slightly more people; they do not make an ignored
post interesting. If reach is the problem, the hook, the first frame and the posting
time are the levers with real weight — and on YouTube specifically, the whole ordering
is in the [YouTube SEO guide](/blog/youtube-seo).

:::faq
Q: How many hashtags should I use?
A: Three to eight on Instagram and TikTok, three to five on YouTube, about three on
LinkedIn, one or two on X and Facebook, and none on Pinterest.
Q: Do hashtags still work?
A: As topic signals and browsable surfaces, yes - modestly. As a reach multiplier, no.
Their weight has fallen everywhere as recommendation systems improved.
Q: Should hashtags go in the caption or the first comment?
A: On Instagram it makes no measurable difference. Choose based on how you want the
caption to read.
Q: Can using too many hashtags hurt?
A: On YouTube, explicitly - more than 60 and every one is ignored, and over-tagging can
remove a video from search. Elsewhere it mostly makes a post look spammy.
:::

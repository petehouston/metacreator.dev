These social media benchmarks are reference bands, not published figures. No platform
releases engagement data, so every benchmark in circulation — including these — is drawn
from samples of public accounts. Use them to place yourself roughly, not to grade
yourself precisely.

## How to read these social media benchmarks

Three caveats that matter more than the numbers:

1. **Account size dominates everything.** A 3,000-follower account and a 300,000-follower
   account are not on the same scale, and comparing them is the most common error in this
   subject.
2. **The formula changes the answer.** Engagement by reach is several times larger than
   engagement by followers for the same post —
   [engagement rate by reach vs by followers](/blog/engagement-rate-by-reach-vs-followers).
3. **Niche shifts the band.** A tight niche out-engages a general audience, consistently.

## Engagement rate bands, by platform and size

Calculated as visible interactions ÷ followers × 100 — the formula an outsider can
verify.

| Platform | Under 10k | 10k-100k | Over 100k |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Instagram | 3-6% | 2-4% | 1-2% |
| TikTok | 6-12% | 5-9% | 3-5% |
| YouTube | 3-6% | 2-4% | 1-3% |
| LinkedIn | 2-5% | 1.5-3% | 1-2% |
| X | 0.5-2% | 0.3-1% | 0.2-0.6% |
| Facebook | 0.5-2% | 0.3-1% | 0.2-0.5% |

Two patterns hold everywhere: **smaller accounts score higher**, and **platforms that
distribute beyond your followers produce higher follower-based ratios** — which is most
of why TikTok's column looks so different from X's.

## Posting cadence

| Platform | Sustainable | What more usually costs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| YouTube long-form | Weekly | Quality, which is the thing that actually compounds |
| YouTube Shorts | 3-7 per week | Little, if cut from existing long-form |
| Instagram | 3-5 per week | Subject drift as ideas run out |
| TikTok | 5-7 per week | Little, if the format is genuinely quick |
| LinkedIn | 2-3 per week | Credibility, if the posts thin out |
| Pinterest | Daily Pins | Nothing — Pinterest rewards volume more than most |

The honest benchmark is not a number: it is **the highest cadence you can hold for six
months on one subject**. See
[a YouTube upload schedule you can keep](/blog/youtube-upload-schedule) and
[a content calendar that survives a week](/blog/content-calendar-guide).

## Content length

| Format | Working range |
| --- | --- |
| Short-form video | 15-45 seconds; completion falls off sharply after |
| YouTube long-form | 8-15 minutes for most subjects; longer if genuinely warranted |
| Instagram caption | 100-300 characters before the fold does the deciding |
| LinkedIn post | 900-1,300 characters, with the hook in the first two lines |
| X post | Under 200 characters travels furthest |
| Pinterest description | Written as prose; up to 800 characters, used for matching |

## Where the honest numbers do exist

Three sources publish something better than a sample, and they are worth knowing about
because they are the only ones you can cite without hedging.

**Your own analytics.** The only figures that are definitionally correct for your
account. Instagram Insights, YouTube Studio and TikTok Analytics all expose reach and
interactions; a quarter of your own data beats any industry average.

**Platform documentation**, for mechanics rather than performance - what a metric counts,
how a feature is distributed, what the limits are. YouTube's
[Creator Academy](https://creatoracademy.youtube.com/) and Meta's
[Business Help Centre](https://www.facebook.com/business/help) are the reference points,
and neither publishes engagement benchmarks because neither wants to.

**Advertising platforms**, for cost data. Ad systems publish real cost and reach figures
because advertisers need them to plan budgets. They describe paid distribution rather
than organic reach, but the audience-size and cost numbers are measured rather than
estimated.

Everything else - including this page - is a sample somebody collected. Treat any
benchmark that arrives without a stated method as a rumour with a decimal point.

## What these numbers cannot tell you

**Whether you are doing well.** A 2% engagement rate on a 50,000-follower account in a
commercial niche with a converting email list is a better business than a 9% rate on a
5,000-follower account that sells nothing.

**What to change.** A benchmark tells you where you sit; it does not tell you why. If the
terms in the tables above are not all familiar, the definitions are in
[the creator metrics glossary](/blog/creator-glossary). The
diagnosis is in the split between reach and response —
[engagement rate](/blog/engagement-rate) — and in which metrics deserve attention at all:
[vanity metrics](/blog/vanity-metrics).

**What a brand will pay.** Rates are negotiated on audience fit and deliverables, not on
a ratio. A benchmark is an argument, not a price.

:::faq
Q: What is a good engagement rate across social media?
A: It depends on platform and size. Roughly 3-6% on Instagram under 10,000 followers,
1-2% above 100,000, with TikTok higher and X and Facebook lower.
Q: Where do social media benchmarks come from?
A: Samples of public accounts, collected by whoever published them. No platform releases
official benchmark data, which is why published figures disagree.
Q: How often should I post?
A: The most you can sustain for six months on one subject. Consistency on a topic beats
frequency across topics.
Q: Should I compare myself to these numbers?
A: Use them to place yourself roughly. The comparison that matters is you against
yourself over the last quarter.
:::

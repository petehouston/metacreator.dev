The fastest way to build a brand color palette from an image is to extract the dominant
colours from a photograph that already feels like your work, then cut the list down to
five and give each one a job. The extraction takes seconds. The cutting down is the
part that produces a brand rather than a swatch.

## Extracting a brand color palette from an image

[[tool:color-palette-extractor]]

Give the extractor an image — a photograph you shot, a thumbnail that performed well, a
product shot — and it returns the dominant colours with their hex codes. Photographs
with a clear subject produce more useful palettes than busy scenes, where the algorithm
averages toward mud.

## Cutting five from twelve

An extracted palette is raw material. A usable brand palette has five roles, and every
colour has to earn one:

| Role | What it does | How many |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary | The colour people associate with you | 1 |
| Secondary | Supports the primary, used for large areas | 1 |
| Accent | Calls to action, highlights, the thing you want clicked | 1 |
| Neutral dark | Text, backgrounds in dark contexts | 1 |
| Neutral light | Backgrounds, space | 1 |

Two rules make this work. **The accent must not be a shade of the primary** — if it is,
nothing stands out. And **at least one neutral must be genuinely neutral**, not a tinted
version of the primary, or every design looks like a monochrome wash.

## Contrast is a requirement, not a preference

A palette that fails contrast is a palette that excludes readers and gets penalised in
accessibility audits. The threshold worth designing to is the WCAG AA level: 4.5:1 for
normal text and 3:1 for large text
([W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html)).

The practical consequence for creators: pale accent colours on white backgrounds fail,
and text on a photograph fails unless you put a solid or scrim behind it. Thumbnails
and Pins are exactly where this goes wrong, because the text sits on the image.

## Using it consistently

A palette only becomes a brand when it is applied the same way every time. Three
places to write it down:

1. **Your editing tools** — save it as a swatch set, not a note.
2. **Your templates** — thumbnail, Story, carousel and Pin templates should already
   carry the colours.
3. **Somewhere you can copy from on a phone**, because half of your posting happens
   there.

The templates are the leverage. A palette in a document is a good intention; a palette
baked into a Story template is a decision you never have to make again — see
[safe zones](/blog/social-media-safe-zones) for the other half of what those templates
should carry.

:::faq
Q: How do I get a colour palette from an image?
A: Run the image through a palette extractor to get the dominant colours as hex codes,
then reduce them to five and assign each a role.
Q: How many colours should a brand palette have?
A: Five is plenty: primary, secondary, accent, and two neutrals. More than that and
nothing is distinctive.
Q: What contrast ratio do I need for text on a colour?
A: 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text meets WCAG AA. Text over a photograph
usually needs a solid backing to reach it.
Q: Should my palette be the same on every platform?
A: Yes. Recognition is the point, and it is built by repetition rather than by variety.

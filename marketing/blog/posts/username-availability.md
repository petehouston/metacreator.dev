Checking username availability one platform at a time is how creators end up with four
different handles and an audience that cannot find them. Check everywhere first, pick a
name that is free in all the places you care about, and claim it on all of them the same
afternoon — even the platforms you do not intend to use yet.

## Checking username availability everywhere at once

[[tool:username-availability-checker]]

The checker does two separable things, and the distinction matters:

**Rules checking**, which is always right. Every network has its own length limits and
allowed characters, and a handle that breaks them is unavailable regardless of who has it.
That is pure computation.

**Availability checking**, which is right where the platform allows it to be read. Some
networks — Instagram, TikTok, X and Threads among them — answer automated requests with a
login wall. For those, the tool says **check manually** and links to the profile rather
than guessing.

That honesty is deliberate. A checker that guesses will eventually report a taken handle
as free, and you will find out after building a brand around it.

## What makes a handle worth keeping

- **Sayable.** If you cannot dictate it over a phone call, it is wrong.
- **Short.** Under 15 characters keeps it usable on the strictest platforms.
- **No numbers substituting for letters.** Misheard, mistyped, and dated.
- **No separators if avoidable.** Underscores and dots are transcribed wrongly.
- **Meaningful without context.** It should survive being read in a comment thread.

## Why one name across platforms matters more than the name itself

Almost any reasonable handle works. What does not work is four different ones, and the
reason is how people actually find you again.

Someone watches a video, remembers roughly who you are, and later types your name into a
different app. If the handle matches, they find you. If it does not, they find a stranger
or nothing, and you have lost a person who was actively looking.

That failure is invisible in analytics. Nobody reports "I tried to find you and could not",
so the cost of inconsistent handles is paid entirely in traffic that never arrives and is
never counted.

There is a second, smaller benefit: a consistent handle makes you mentionable. A podcast
host, a newsletter writer or a person recommending you in a comment can write your name
once and have it work everywhere - which is the cheapest distribution there is.

## Claim it everywhere, immediately

Handles are free and finite. Claiming your name on a platform you have no plans for costs
ten minutes and prevents the situation where you expand to that platform two years later
and someone else has your name.

## When your first choice is gone

It usually is. The patterns that generate available handles without wrecking them:

| Pattern | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Name + subject | `samonespresso` |
| Verb + subject | `buildingboats` |
| Two-word compound | `cleartablerule` |
| Prefix | `hey`, `real`, `the` — used sparingly |

What to avoid: appending a number, adding a year, or adopting a different handle per
platform. The last one is the most common and the most expensive, because discovery on one
platform stops helping you on the others.

## Changing a handle later

Possible everywhere, and costly in the same ways: old links break, past mentions do not
follow, and a released handle can be claimed by someone else — after which your former
audience typing the old name lands on a stranger's profile.

The exception is anything built on a stable identifier rather than a handle. YouTube's
channel ID never changes, which is why feeds, subscribe links and integrations should use
it — [how to find a YouTube channel ID](/blog/find-youtube-channel-id).

To cite a YouTube video you need five things: who uploaded it, the upload date, the
video title, the site name, and the URL. Every citation style arranges those five
differently. Here is each arrangement, plus what to do when the uploader is a channel
rather than a person.

## The five fields, and where to find them

| Field | Where |
| --- | --- |
| Uploader | The channel name under the video. Use the real name if it is known, otherwise the channel name |
| Upload date | Under the video, or in the page metadata for the exact date |
| Title | Exactly as written, including capitalisation |
| Site | "YouTube" |
| URL | The full watch URL. Add a timestamp if you are citing a moment |

## How to cite a YouTube video in each style

**APA**

```text
Uploader. (Year, Month Day). Title of video [Video]. YouTube. https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ID
```

APA uses the uploader as the author — the channel name is acceptable when no personal
name is given, and a real name is used with the channel name in square brackets when
both are known.

**MLA**

```text
"Title of Video." YouTube, uploaded by Uploader, Day Month Year, www.youtube.com/watch?v=ID.
```

MLA leads with the title in quotation marks and credits the uploader after the site.

**Chicago (notes-bibliography)**

```text
Uploader, "Title of Video," YouTube video, duration, Month Day, Year, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ID.
```

Chicago wants the running time, which is one of the fields a citation generator saves
you from counting by hand.

**Harvard**

```text
Uploader (Year) Title of video. Available at: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ID (Accessed: Day Month Year).
```

Harvard is the style that wants an access date, because it assumes web content moves.

## Why the fields are harder than they look

Two of the five fields are where citations go wrong, and both are avoidable.

**The date.** The date shown under a video is relative once it is older than a few
days - "3 years ago" is not a citation. The exact upload date is in the page metadata,
and it is what every style expects. Guessing from a relative label produces a citation
that cannot be verified, which defeats the purpose.

**The title.** Titles get edited. A video cited under an old title is hard for a
reader to find, and titles are also frequently written in a capitalisation that your
style guide would change - MLA and Chicago want title case, APA wants sentence case
for the video title. Copy the title exactly, then apply the style's capitalisation
rather than retyping from memory.

The third quiet trap is the URL. Share links carry tracking parameters
(`?si=` and similar) that are specific to the person who copied them. Strip everything
after the video ID; a citation should not carry a referral tag.

## Citing a specific moment

If your point rests on something said at 4:12, cite that moment rather than the whole
video. A timestamped URL is a legitimate citation target and it saves your reader
scrubbing through forty minutes.

## The awkward cases

**A channel with no personal name.** Use the channel name as the author. Do not invent
a person.

**A re-upload.** Cite what you actually watched, and say so if the original is
identifiable. Citing the original when you watched a copy is a small dishonesty that
occasionally becomes a large one.

**A video that has since been deleted.** Keep your access date and, where the style
allows, note that it is no longer available. Archived copies are worth linking if a
reputable archive has one.

**A private or unlisted video.** Cite it as personal communication if it is not
publicly retrievable — a citation that nobody else can follow is not a citation.

:::faq
Q: Who is the author when citing a YouTube video?
A: The uploader. Use a real name if the channel gives one, otherwise the channel name
itself. Do not attribute a video to someone who merely appears in it.
Q: Do I need the upload date or the access date?
A: APA, MLA and Chicago use the upload date. Harvard also wants the access date,
because web content changes.
Q: How do I cite a specific time in a YouTube video?
A: Use a timestamped URL, and follow your style guide on whether the time also appears
in the in-text citation.
Q: Can I cite a deleted YouTube video?
A: You can cite what you watched, with the access date, and note that it is no longer
available. An archived copy is worth linking where one exists.
:::

A readability score measures sentence length and word length, and infers difficulty from
them. It does not measure clarity, accuracy or whether your point survived. Used with that
in mind it is a genuinely useful signal; used as a target it produces writing that scores
well and says nothing.

## What a readability score actually counts

The common ones — Flesch Reading Ease, Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level, Gunning Fog — differ in
their arithmetic and agree in their inputs:

| Input | What it stands for |
| --- | --- |
| Words per sentence | Structural complexity |
| Syllables per word | Vocabulary difficulty |

That is it. Neither input knows what your words mean. "The cat sat on the mat" and "The
tax was set on the mat" score identically, and one of them is nonsense.

## What to aim for

For social copy and most web writing, a grade level of around 7 to 9 — a Flesch Reading
Ease in the 60s. That is not a dumbing-down target; it is the level at which a reader on a
phone, distracted, gets your point on the first pass.

Two contexts where a higher grade level is fine: writing for a specialist audience where
the long words are the precise ones, and writing where the reader has already committed —
a long-form article rather than a caption.

## How to improve a score without wrecking the writing

The formulas reward shorter sentences and shorter words, so the honest improvements are
the ones that would improve the writing anyway:

- **Split sentences that contain two ideas.** Usually the one with a semicolon.
- **Cut hedges.** "It could be argued that" is five words of nothing.
- **Prefer the plain word** where it is genuinely equivalent — "use" over "utilise". Not
  where it is not: "monetization" has no short synonym and replacing it loses meaning.
- **Cut adverbs.** Most are doing no work.

What not to do is chop every sentence to eight words. Uniformly short sentences score
beautifully and read like a fire alarm. Rhythm comes from variation.

## Where it matters most

**Captions and posts**, where the reader is scanning and the fold is early —
[Instagram caption length](/blog/instagram-caption-length) and
[where LinkedIn puts See more](/blog/linkedin-see-more).

**Scripts**, where difficult sentences are worse than on the page because the listener
cannot re-read. Reading a script aloud finds problems no formula catches —
[words per minute speaking](/blog/words-per-minute-speaking).

**Headlines**, where the constraint is severe and the score is nearly meaningless because
the sample is too small — [how to write headlines](/blog/how-to-write-headlines) is the
better instrument there.

## The honest limits

A readability score cannot tell you whether the writing is any good, whether the argument
holds, or whether the reader cares. It is a smoke detector: useful when it goes off, not
evidence that dinner is delicious.

The original research behind the Flesch formulas, and their intended use, is documented by
the [Plain Language](https://www.plainlanguage.gov/) programme — which is also a better
guide to clear writing than any score.

:::faq
Q: What is a good readability score?
A: Around grade 7 to 9, or a Flesch Reading Ease in the 60s, for social and general web
writing.
Q: Does readability affect SEO?
A: Not directly as a ranking factor. It affects whether people stay and understand, which
does show up in the metrics search engines can measure.
Q: Can a readability score be wrong?
A: It can only be misapplied. It measures sentence and word length, so nonsense written in
short words scores well.
Q: Should I write to hit a target score?
A: No. Use it to find sentences worth revisiting, then revise for meaning rather than for
the number.
:::

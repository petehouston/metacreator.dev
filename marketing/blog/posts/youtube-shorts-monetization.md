YouTube Shorts monetization works differently from long-form, and the difference is
structural rather than a matter of scale. Ads between Shorts go into a Creator Pool;
creators receive 45% of the revenue allocated to them based on their share of views
([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/72902)). Long-form pays 55%
of the ad revenue on your own watch page.

## YouTube Shorts monetization uses a different mechanism

| | Long-form | Shorts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Revenue source | Ads on your watch page | Ads in the Shorts feed, pooled |
| Creator share | 55% of net revenue | 45% of the pool allocated to you |
| Allocation | Per video | By share of total Shorts views |
| Music licensing | Not deducted this way | Deducted from the pool before allocation |

The pool model is the important part. Your Shorts income is not "the ads shown next to
your Short" — it is a proportion of a large shared pot, sized by your share of all
Shorts views in your region, with music licensing costs taken out of the pot before
anyone is paid.

That is why a Short with a million views can earn a fraction of what a long-form video
with a hundred thousand views earns.

## What that means in practice

**Shorts RPM is dramatically lower than long-form RPM.** Not slightly — often by an
order of magnitude. Any comparison between the two that treats a view as a view is
misleading, and the metric to compare is
[RPM, not CPM](/blog/rpm-vs-cpm).

**Using licensed music reduces what you are paid**, because licensing comes out of the
pool first. Original or library audio avoids that deduction.

**Volume does not fix the ratio.** Making more Shorts increases your share of the pool
proportionally; it does not change the rate.

## Why the pool exists at all

It is worth understanding the reasoning, because it explains why this is unlikely to
change. A Shorts feed shows one video after another with ads interspersed - an ad is
not attached to a particular video the way a pre-roll is attached to a watch page.
There is no honest way to say which Short "earned" a given ad impression, so YouTube
pools the revenue and divides it by view share.

The music deduction has a similar logic. A large share of Shorts use commercial music,
and someone has to pay the rights holders. Taking it from the pool before allocation
means creators using licensed tracks are effectively subsidised by those who are not -
which is also why using original audio improves your share.

Neither decision is arbitrary, and neither is likely to be reversed. Planning a channel
on the assumption that Shorts payouts will eventually approach long-form rates is
planning on a change nobody has announced.

## What Shorts are actually good for

Not ad revenue. Three things they do well:

1. **Discovery.** The Shorts feed reaches people who have never heard of you, which is
   the single hardest thing for a new channel to buy.
2. **Testing.** A hook or a subject can be validated in a 30-second Short before you
   spend a week on a long-form video about it.
3. **Qualifying for the Partner Programme.** The Shorts route is 1,000 subscribers plus
   10 million qualified Shorts views in 90 days
   ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/72851)) — a large number,
   but a real route.

## The strategy that follows from the numbers

If income is the aim, Shorts are the funnel and long-form is the business. The channels
that make this work use Shorts to acquire an audience and long-form to earn from it —
and the connective tissue is that the Short has to be *about* something the long-form
covers, so a viewer who liked one has a reason to watch the other.

The failure mode is a channel that posts Shorts about whatever is trending and long-form
about its actual subject. Those are two audiences, and neither converts into the other.

Practical sequencing:

- Cut Shorts **from** long-form you already made, so the audiences match.
- Make the Short answer a question the long-form answers in more depth.
- Keep the subject narrow enough that the recommendation system can categorise both.

:::faq
Q: How much do YouTube Shorts pay?
A: Far less per view than long-form. Shorts revenue comes from a shared Creator Pool
with a 45% creator share allocated by view share, rather than 55% of ads on your own
watch page.
Q: Why is my Shorts RPM so low?
A: Because of the pool model and, if you use licensed music, because licensing costs
are deducted from the pool before allocation.
Q: Do Shorts views count toward the 4,000 watch hours?
A: No. They count only toward the separate 10-million-Shorts-views route to the Partner
Programme.
Q: Are Shorts worth making if they pay so little?
A: For discovery and testing, yes. As a revenue stream on their own, rarely - they work
best as the entry point to long-form.
:::

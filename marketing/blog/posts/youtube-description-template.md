Here is the YouTube description template, before any explanation, because that is
what you came for. It is ordered by what a viewer actually sees: three lines above
the fold, then the parts that do work for search and for you.

```text
[One sentence restating the promise of the video, in the viewer's words.]
[One sentence on what they will be able to do afterwards.]
[The single most useful link, if there is one.]

CHAPTERS
00:00 Cold open
00:42 The problem
03:15 The fix
07:30 What to do next

RESOURCES
- Thing mentioned at 03:15 — https://example.com/?utm_source=youtube&utm_medium=video&utm_campaign=video-slug
- Tool used in this video — https://example.com/tool

ABOUT
[Two lines on the channel. Not five.]

#relevanthashtag #secondone #third
```

That is the whole thing. The rest of this page is why each block is where it is.

## The three lines of your YouTube description that are visible

YouTube shows roughly the first two or three lines before "Show more". Everything
above that fold is read by people deciding whether to keep watching; everything below
is read by almost nobody and by YouTube.

So those lines carry the promise, not your Patreon. The most common mistake in this
field is a channel that opens every description with the same forty words of
boilerplate — spending its only visible space on something a returning viewer already
knows and a new one does not care about.

## Chapters, because one video can answer several questions

Timestamps in the description become chapters, and chapters get their own presence in
search — a viewer can be dropped at the exact minute that answers them. For anything
over about four minutes covering more than one point, this is the highest-return
block in the template.

The rules are strict enough to be worth checking: the list must start at `00:00`, be
in ascending order, and have at least three entries.

## Links you can actually measure

A raw link in a description tells you nothing about which video sent the click. Tagged
links tell you everything, and cost nothing:

[[tool:utm-link-builder]]

Use one consistent scheme — source `youtube`, medium `video`, campaign the video's
own slug — and your analytics can finally answer "which video drives signups". The
scheme and the naming discipline are in
[UTM parameters for creators](/blog/utm-parameters-for-creators).

## Hashtags at the bottom, three of them

Hashtags in the description render as links above the video title. YouTube shows up
to three of them, ignores every hashtag on a video that carries more than 60, and may
remove an over-tagged video from search altogether
([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6390658)).

Three relevant ones. There is no version of this where thirty is better.

## How long should a description be?

Long enough to contain the blocks above; the ceiling is 5,000 characters
([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12948449)) and almost
nobody should approach it. A description padded with keyword lists is the 2012
playbook, and it reads as spam to the only audience that matters.

| Block | Length | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Visible lines | 2-3 sentences | Keep the viewer |
| Chapters | One line each | Search surfaces, navigation |
| Resources | The links that matter | Clicks you can measure |
| About | Two lines | Context for a new viewer |
| Hashtags | Three | Modest discovery, visible above the title |

## Filling it in without rewriting it every time

The template is only useful if it survives contact with a weekly upload schedule. Two
habits make that work.

**Write the visible lines last.** They restate the promise of the finished video, and
you do not reliably know what that is until it is edited. Draft them from the video's
own hook rather than from the plan you started with.

**Keep the boilerplate in one file, not in your head.** The ABOUT block and the
standing links change perhaps twice a year. Everything above them changes every
video. Treating them as two different things stops you retyping — and stops the
boilerplate creeping upward into the visible lines, which is how it usually gets
there.

If you publish on a schedule, build the descriptions when you build the schedule
rather than at upload time; the
[YouTube upload schedule](/blog/youtube-upload-schedule) piece covers planning the
batch.

## The channel description is a different field

The "About" section on your channel is not the same thing as a video description, has
its own fold, and is worth writing once properly rather than pasting from a video.

:::faq
Q: How many characters can a YouTube description have?
A: 5,000. Only the first two or three lines are visible before "Show more", so treat
those as the description and the rest as supporting material.
Q: Do descriptions affect YouTube search ranking?
A: They give YouTube text to understand the video, and the visible lines affect
whether someone keeps watching - which matters more. They are not a place to stuff
keywords.
Q: Should the same description be used on every video?
A: Only the boilerplate block at the bottom. The visible lines and the chapters must
be specific to the video, or the most valuable part of the field is wasted.
Q: Where do chapters come from?
A: The timestamp list in the description, starting at 00:00, in ascending order, with
at least three entries. YouTube builds the chapters from that automatically.
:::

Build the chapter list first — it is the block most descriptions are missing:

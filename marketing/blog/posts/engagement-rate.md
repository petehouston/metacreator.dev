Engagement rate is the share of an audience that did something with a post. There is no
single formula — there are four in common use, they produce different numbers from the
same post, and the one you quote should depend on what you are trying to describe.

## The four engagement rate formulas

| Formula | Calculation | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| By followers | (interactions ÷ followers) × 100 | Comparing accounts of similar size |
| By reach | (interactions ÷ accounts reached) × 100 | Judging whether a post worked |
| By impressions | (interactions ÷ impressions) × 100 | The most conservative number |
| By views | (interactions ÷ views) × 100 | Video-first platforms |

"Interactions" usually means likes plus comments plus saves plus shares — but which of
those you can see depends on the platform and on whether it is your own account.

## Why they disagree, and by how much

Reach is always smaller than follower count for most accounts, so **engagement by reach
is a larger number than engagement by followers** — often several times larger. Neither
is wrong. They answer different questions:

- *"Of the people who follow me, how many respond?"* → by followers.
- *"Of the people who saw this, how many responded?"* → by reach.

That difference is also why comparing your engagement rate to a figure you read
somewhere is usually meaningless. If they used reach and you used followers, you are
comparing different measurements with the same name. The full explanation is in
[engagement rate by reach vs by followers](/blog/engagement-rate-by-reach-vs-followers).

## Which one to quote a brand

**By followers**, unless the brand asks otherwise, and say which formula you used.

Two reasons. It is the one a brand can verify from outside — they can count your
followers and your visible likes and comments without access to your analytics. And it
is the more conservative number, which means you never have to explain later why the
figure in your media kit was flattering.

Quoting reach-based engagement without labelling it is the most common way creators
lose a second deal.

## What counts as an interaction

Platform by platform, this is where comparisons quietly break:

- **Likes and comments** — visible everywhere, countable from outside.
- **Saves and shares** — usually the strongest signals, visible only to the account
  owner on most platforms.
- **Video views** — not engagement. A view is exposure, not a response.
- **Follows from a post** — real, rarely available.

If you include saves and shares in your own figure and someone else does not, the
numbers are not comparable. Say what you included.

## What a good number looks like

Benchmarks vary by platform, by follower count and by niche, and no platform publishes
official figures — which is why any single number quoted as "the average" should be
treated as somebody's sample rather than a fact.

The stable, verifiable patterns:

- **Smaller accounts have higher engagement rates.** Consistently, on every platform.
  A 5,000-follower account with 6% is unremarkable; a 500,000-follower account with 6%
  is extraordinary.
- **Video formats out-engage static ones** on the platforms that push video.
- **Niche accounts out-engage general ones**, because the audience self-selected.

## Measuring it over a window, not a post

A single post's engagement rate is noise. Something was posted at an odd hour, or
happened to catch a wave, or landed on a day the audience was elsewhere. The number
worth acting on is a **median across a window** - the last twelve posts, or the last
ninety days.

Use the median rather than the mean. One post that unexpectedly travelled will drag a
mean upward and describe a typical week inaccurately; the median tells you what
normally happens, which is what a brand is buying and what you are trying to improve.

Two windows are worth keeping:

- **Rolling median, last 12 posts.** Your current state. This is the number to quote.
- **Same window a quarter ago.** The comparison that tells you the direction.

Anything shorter than about ten posts is not a measurement, it is a mood.

## Where the platforms differ

The formula is arithmetic and transfers everywhere. What does not transfer is the
denominator you can actually see and the interactions that exist.

Instagram gives an account owner reach and saves; an outsider sees only likes and
comments. TikTok publishes view counts on every video, which makes engagement-by-views
the natural measure and also the least flattering one. YouTube's public interactions
are thin - likes and comments against views - and its meaningful engagement signals,
watch time and retention, are private to the channel. Meta's own
[Business Help Centre](https://www.facebook.com/business/help) documents which metrics
its platforms expose, and the differences are the reason a cross-platform engagement
comparison is always approximate.

Which is why a cross-platform engagement comparison is always approximate, and why the
useful comparison is you against yourself over time.

## Using it honestly

Engagement rate is a ratio, and ratios are easy to manipulate. Three ways it gets
gamed, all visible to anyone paying attention:

**Engagement pods.** Reciprocal liking inflates the numerator with people who do not
care. Brands increasingly check whether comments are substantive.

**Buying followers.** This *lowers* engagement rate, which is why suspiciously low
engagement on a large account is a warning sign rather than a small one.

**Cherry-picking the post.** A media kit quoting the best post of the year is quoting a
best case. Use a median across a recent window and say what the window was.

:::faq
Q: How do you calculate engagement rate?
A: Interactions divided by an audience number, times 100. The audience number is
followers, reach, impressions or views - and which you use changes the answer.
Q: What is a good engagement rate?
A: It depends on platform, size and niche. Smaller accounts consistently score higher,
so compare yourself to accounts of similar size rather than to a global average.
Q: Should I use reach or followers?
A: Reach to judge whether a post worked; followers when quoting a figure to a brand,
because it is verifiable from outside and more conservative.
Q: Do views count as engagement?
A: No. A view is exposure. Engagement is what someone did after seeing it.
:::

The cheapest YouTube keyword research is also the most trustworthy: YouTube's own
search box completes what people are typing, and those completions come from real
searches rather than a third party's estimate. No subscription, no invented volume
figures, and it works on a channel with four subscribers.

## Why autocomplete beats a volume number

A paid tool gives you "1,900 searches a month". That figure is modelled — it is
somebody's estimate of a number YouTube has never published. Autocomplete gives you
something smaller and more useful: proof that a phrase is typed often enough for
YouTube to suggest it, in YouTube's own words, in the exact order people type them.

For a small channel, that is the better input anyway. You are not choosing between a
1,900-a-month term and a 2,100-a-month term. You are choosing between a phrase people
actually use and a phrase you invented at your desk.

## The YouTube keyword research method, in four passes

### 1. Seed

Start with the plain phrase for your subject — the words a stranger would use, not
your niche's jargon. "Sourdough starter", not "levain maintenance".

### 2. Expand

Run the seed, then run it again with each letter of the alphabet appended: `sourdough
starter a`, `sourdough starter b`, and so on. Each pass returns a different slice of
the suggestion tree. Twenty-six quick runs is a surprisingly complete map of what
people ask about a subject.

Also try these prefixes, which surface intent rather than topic:

| Prefix | What it finds |
| --- | --- |
| how to … | Tutorial demand — the easiest to serve well |
| why is my … | Problems, which convert into loyal viewers |
| best … for … | Comparison demand |
| … vs … | Explicit comparisons |
| can you … | Yes/no questions, ideal for Shorts |

### 3. Sort by what you can win

You now have a hundred phrases. Most of them are not for you yet. Sort them by
specificity: a four-word question is a video a small channel can rank for, and a
two-word category is one it cannot. The two-word terms are what you eventually win by
having covered the four-word ones.

### 4. Check the shape of the results

Search the phrase on YouTube and read the first screen. Two things tell you whether
to make the video:

- **Are the top results actually answering it?** If they are tangential, the query is
  underserved and that is an opening.
- **How old and how large?** A page of results from million-subscriber channels is a
  hard fight. A page with mid-size channels and a mediocre thumbnail is an invitation.

## Vocabulary research: what competitors think their video is about

Autocomplete tells you what viewers type. Tags tell you what other creators think
they made. Both are useful and they are not the same thing.

[[tool:youtube-tag-extractor]]

Read a competitor's tags for vocabulary, not to copy — YouTube says tags play a
minimal role in discovery
([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/146402)), so copying them
achieves nothing. What you are mining is phrasing you had not thought of.
See [how to see the tags on any YouTube video](/blog/how-to-see-tags-on-a-youtube-video).

## A worked example

Seed: `link preview`.

1. Autocomplete returns `link preview not showing`, `link preview image size`,
   `link preview generator`.
2. `why is my link preview` returns `not updating`, `showing old image`, `wrong`.
3. The problem queries are the opportunity: they are specific, they have obvious
   answers, and the person searching is stuck rather than browsing.
4. That becomes a video titled around the problem — and, on this site, the post
   [link preview not showing](/blog/link-preview-not-showing).

The same tree gives you a series rather than a single video, which is the real point.
One seed, properly expanded, is a month of uploads that reinforce each other.

## What to do with the leftovers

Phrases you cannot win yet are not waste. They are the pillar you build toward: cover
fifteen specific questions on a subject and the general term becomes reachable,
because you now have the one thing a new channel cannot buy — evidence that you cover
the subject. That logic is the whole structure of the
[YouTube SEO guide](/blog/youtube-seo).

:::faq
Q: Is YouTube keyword research possible without a paid tool?
A: Yes. Autocomplete is the primary source and it is free. Paid tools mainly add
modelled volume estimates, which are useful at scale and unnecessary when you are
choosing between specific questions.
Q: How many keywords should one video target?
A: One primary phrase, plus whatever related phrasings appear naturally. A video
built to serve three unrelated queries usually serves none of them well.
Q: Do YouTube tags help with keyword targeting?
A: Barely. YouTube states they play a minimal role except for commonly misspelled
terms. Put the work into the title, the first lines of the description, and what you
actually say in the first thirty seconds.
Q: Where does autocomplete data come from?
A: YouTube's own search completions - what people are typing. It is not a projection,
which is why it comes without volume numbers attached.
:::

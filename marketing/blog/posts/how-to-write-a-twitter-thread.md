To write a Twitter thread that gets read, treat it as a sequence where each post earns
the next one — not as an article cut into 280-character pieces. The first post carries
almost all of the outcome, and the second post is where nearly everyone leaves.

## How to write a Twitter thread: the structure

| Post | Job |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Stand alone. Make a claim specific enough to be interesting without the rest |
| 2 | Justify the click. Deliver something immediately, before any setup |
| 3-n | One idea each, in the order someone would ask about them |
| Last | The point, restated. Not a plug |

**Post one is the whole thread** as far as distribution goes. It is what appears in
timelines, what gets quoted, and what everything else depends on. "A thread 🧵" as an
opening is a promise with no content, and it is competing against posts that made their
point immediately.

## Where to break

Break at the end of a thought, never mid-clause. A post that ends halfway through a
sentence forces the reader to continue and is exactly the mechanic that makes threads
feel manipulative.

Doing this by hand is tedious and error-prone, particularly with the weighted character
counting X uses — URLs count as a fixed length, some scripts count double.

## What makes people stay

**Front-load every post.** Each one is read in a timeline in isolation, so each needs its
own first three words to work.

**Vary the shape.** A thread of eight identical-looking paragraphs is skimmed. A short
post between two longer ones creates a rhythm that is genuinely easier to read.

**Use an image where an image is faster.** A screenshot, a chart or a table conveys in
one glance what three posts would explain — and attachments cost no characters.

[[tool:social-image-resizer]]

Sizes and the timeline crop are in
[Twitter image sizes for X](/blog/twitter-image-size). Remember that the crop is
aggressive: a screenshot of a paragraph becomes an unreadable band.

**Number them, or do not.** "1/12" sets an expectation, which helps a good thread and
warns people off a long one. Both are defensible; what is not is numbering a thread and
then adding four more posts.

## How to end

With the point, stated plainly. The last post is the one people quote, so it should
contain the thing you want quoted.

A single link or a single ask afterwards is acceptable if the thread delivered
something first. Two asks is one too many, and a thread that ends by selling something
teaches your audience not to open the next one.

## Threads on Threads

The same structure works on Threads with one adjustment: the platform surfaces
conversation, so ending with something answerable does more there than ending with a
conclusion. The limits differ too —
[the Threads character limit](/blog/threads-character-limit) — and the full comparison is
in [how to post on X and Threads](/blog/how-to-post-on-x). X's own counting rules are
documented in its
[developer documentation](https://developer.x.com/en/docs/counting-characters).

:::faq
Q: How do I write a good Twitter thread?
A: Make the first post work alone, deliver something concrete in the second, keep one
idea per post, and end with the point rather than a plug.
Q: How long should a thread be?
A: As long as it stays interesting. Most good threads are five to ten posts; beyond that
the drop-off is steep.
Q: Should I number the posts in a thread?
A: Optional. Numbering sets an expectation, which helps a strong thread and discourages
people from starting a long one.
Q: How do I split long text into a thread?
A: At sentence boundaries, respecting X's weighted character counting. A splitter does
both without the manual counting.
:::

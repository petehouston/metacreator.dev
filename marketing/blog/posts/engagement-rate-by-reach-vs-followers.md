Engagement rate by reach divides interactions by the number of accounts that actually
saw the post. Engagement rate by followers divides the same interactions by your total
follower count. For most accounts the first number is several times the second, and
neither is wrong — they answer different questions.

## Engagement rate by reach, and by followers

| | By reach | By followers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Formula | interactions ÷ accounts reached × 100 | interactions ÷ followers × 100 |
| Question | Did this post work? | How responsive is my audience? |
| Needs | Your own analytics | Nothing — anyone can calculate it |
| Typical size | Higher, often 2-5× | Lower |
| Best for | Judging a post | Quoting to a brand |

## Why they diverge

Reach is almost always smaller than follower count. Not every follower sees every post,
and on platforms that distribute beyond your following, some of your reach is people who
do not follow you at all.

That produces two effects that pull in opposite directions:

- **A smaller denominator raises the reach-based number.** If 20% of your followers see
  a post, the reach-based rate is roughly five times the follower-based one.
- **Non-follower reach lowers it**, because those viewers are less likely to interact
  than people who chose to follow you.

On Instagram the first effect dominates. On TikTok, where a large share of reach is
non-follower, the second one bites — which is why
[TikTok engagement rates](/blog/good-engagement-rate-tiktok) look strange next to
[Instagram's](/blog/good-engagement-rate-instagram).

## Which one to use

**To judge a post: by reach.** It isolates the question you are actually asking — of the
people who saw this, how many responded? A post with low reach and high reach-based
engagement is a good post with a distribution problem, and that is a completely
different fix from a bad post.

**To quote a brand: by followers.** It is verifiable without your analytics, it is the
more conservative number, and it is what they will calculate anyway.

**Always label which you used.** An unlabelled engagement rate in a media kit is the
single most common way creators lose credibility on a second deal — the brand runs their
own number, gets something much lower, and concludes you inflated it.

## Comparing yourself to anyone else

You cannot see another account's reach, so any external comparison has to be
follower-based on visible interactions only. That is fine, as long as you apply the same
restriction to yourself rather than comparing your reach-based internal figure to their
follower-based external one.

The method:

1. Take their last twelve posts, excluding anything obviously exceptional.
2. Likes plus comments, divided by follower count, times 100.
3. Take the median.
4. Do exactly the same to yourself, ignoring your saves and reach data.

## Why your own number moved

If your engagement rate changed without your content changing, one of these is usually
why:

- **Follower growth.** The denominator grew; attention did not. Normal.
- **A viral post.** Reach spiked, interactions did not keep pace, reach-based rate fell.
- **Format change.** A shift from carousels to single images changes both numbers.
- **You switched formulas** without noticing — the most common cause of a dramatic
  overnight "change".

The full set of formulas is in [engagement rate](/blog/engagement-rate), the reference
bands are in [social media benchmarks](/blog/social-media-benchmarks), and Meta documents
which metrics its apps expose in its
[Business Help Centre](https://www.facebook.com/business/help).

:::faq
Q: Should I use reach or followers for engagement rate?
A: Reach to judge whether a post worked, followers when quoting a figure externally.
Label whichever you use.
Q: Why is my engagement rate by reach so much higher?
A: Because reach is smaller than your follower count - often much smaller. The same
interactions divided by a smaller number produce a larger percentage.
Q: Which engagement rate do brands use?
A: Follower-based, on visible likes and comments, because it is what they can verify
without access to your analytics.
Q: Is a high reach-based engagement rate good if reach is low?
A: Yes, and it is a useful diagnosis: the content works and the distribution does not.
Those are different problems with different fixes.
:::
